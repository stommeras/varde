# Varde spec

Nothing here is final. Agents made most of these choices while building the game, and any of them can change when another choice gives a better result. The PR that changes one updates this file and shows why.

## The game

You land on an unknown island with a blank map. Somewhere on the true summit stands an old varde, and a trail of smaller vardes marks the way up. You follow the trail, explore off it when you like, and the map fills in as you go. Reaching the summit varde ends the run.

The game is calm. There are no supplies, no combat and no way to fail. Curiosity about the next varde and the unmapped land is the only pressure. Each run is a new island from a new seed, and a run lasts about 20 to 40 minutes.

A run starts from a title screen with "Ny øy" and a small, optional seed field below it. A release build shows its version, such as "v0.4.0", small in the bottom-right corner of the title screen and the pause menu, so a bug report can name the build. A local build has no version and shows nothing there. An empty field gives a random seed, a number is the seed itself, and any other text is hashed into one. Behind the menu, the island from seed "varde" pans by at zoom 256 and 10 pixels per second. The view starts at a random point toward the edge of the land, heads in toward the middle, and turns back in a slow arc whenever it drifts out past the land. A still image of the whole grid, `game/backdrop.png`, shows from the first frame. `godot -s game/render_backdrop.gd` renders it through the game camera ahead of time. Meanwhile the island generates on a thread, and once it is ready the still fades out over the live terrain at the same spot, so the water moves. Later visits to the title screen reuse the island and show it live at once. Render the still again after a change to the generator or the look, or it won't match the live island when it fades. The game icon, `icon.png`, is the player's varde on the same island's summit, seen from the west with grass, beach and sea behind it. `godot -s game/render_icon.gd` renders it the same way, so render it again after the same changes. While the run's island generates, the backdrop stays on screen under a 60% veil of dark sea, with "Kartlegger øya", a progress bar and the name of the running step. The bar fills by each step's typical share of the time, and within a step by the time since it started. Once the island shows, Escape pauses the run behind a menu with "Fortsett" and "Avslutt". "Avslutt" ends the run and goes back to the title screen, and there is no saving yet.

## Core mechanics

**Line of sight.** You see a radius around you, and higher terrain blocks your view. The radius is 40 cells at sea level and grows to about 120 cells at summit height, so choosing which hill to climb is the main exploration decision. Land you have seen stays revealed and never fades back into fog. Milestone 4 kept these radii. Letting tall land show from further away was dropped: the camera looks down, so a peak only looks tall once the player is on it, and the extra land would show on the map but hardly on screen.

**Automatic mapping.** The map fills in at two levels:

- Walking marks land in your line of sight as seen. Seen land gets coastlines, rivers and rough outlines on the map.
- Surveying from a peak marks land as surveyed. Surveyed land also gets contour lines.

**Peaks and surveying.** The peaks stand on 6 to 9 massifs that the generator raises on purpose (generator step 1), and only massifs reach the snow. The generator stores every top that stands at least 50% of the summit's height and rises at least 20% of it above the saddle to any higher top. The massifs stand in ranges with saddles at about half the summit's height, and at 35% only 2 to 4 of their tops counted. At 25%, seed 2 had 3 peaks, and four of seeds 0 to 9 counted a lone knoll at 29 to 58% of the summit's height, which the height cut now drops. Seeds 0 to 9 give 6 to 12 peaks, aiming for 5 to 10, with more on the islands that have room for more massifs. The player can walk from the start up to every peak's top, or a cell beside it (generator step 5). Each peak has a survey zone with a radius of 5 cells. Within 10 cells of an unsurveyed top, a thin ring floats over the zone's edge, so the player can find the top. It hangs 0.4 world units above the highest ground along that edge, bobs 0.15 units every 2 seconds, and pulses by dithering its light middle between two snow steps. It is about 4 pixels thick, light in the middle and dark at the edges, so it shows against snow and rock, and it dithers in and out over half a second. A circle painted on the ground came first, but it wobbled with every bump in the slope, and a width set in cells grew to 8 pixels where a steep slope faces the camera. With a 6-cell zone and no ring, the prompt came up well below the top of a steep peak, so the zone shrank to 3. Once the figure walked up to the top itself, 3 cells only made the player hunt for the top's few cells, so it grew to 5. Standing in a zone shows "Kartlegg toppen". Pressing F walks the figure up to a cell beside the top along a path of steps it can take, a few cells at walking speed, and then plays a short survey animation. Before the walk, a survey started at the zone's edge happened below the top. Surveying uses the same line of sight with twice the walking radius and marks everything it reaches as surveyed. The animation ends with your character stacking a varde on the peak, so every surveyed peak gets one of your vardes on the map. Your vardes have no other effect. The survey takes about 6.5 seconds. For 5 seconds the camera pulls out over 2 seconds from zoom 40 to 120, three times the walking view, and turns once around, clockwise, in four quarter turns, so the player sees the fog clear on every side of the peak. The figure faces away from the camera and turns with it, so it looks out over each side as the camera shows it. A figure turning smoothly once around with the camera only swung within a quarter turn of facing the camera, and didn't read as looking around. Each side holds still for 0.85 seconds before the next 0.4-second turn, and the last turn ends as the look does, back at the heading the survey started from. Q and E do nothing during a survey. A smooth spin would hold the camera off its pixel grid for the whole 5 seconds, where the dither and the edges shimmer. Meanwhile the fog clears from the surveyed land in a ring that spreads from the peak over 4.5 seconds. Then the figure turns to the top, and over 1.5 seconds the varde goes up stone by stone as the camera comes back to zoom 40. A pull-out to 80 over 3 seconds looked tame and hurried, and at 160 the figure and the varde shrank to a few pixels. Each peak can be surveyed once. The varde is 12 flattened, faceted spheres in six layers of three, three, two, two, one and one stones, a little shorter than the figure, lit by the figure's shader in a stone ramp of every other rock step. Its shape comes from the peak's cell, so a peak always gets the same varde. It stands on the top cell, since the walk stops a cell short of it. Only where every cell around the top is cliff does the figure stand on the top, and then the varde goes up a cell away. The prompt reads "[F] Kartlegg toppen" in snow-white text with a dark outline at the bottom of the screen and fades in and out over 0.2 seconds. It is the first HUD element.

**The summit is hard to find.** Three to five contender peaks have heights within about 10 to 15% of each other, and only some of them reach snow. The generator rescales only the contenders into that band and leaves the smaller peaks and hills alone, which can be half the summit's height or lower. From below you can't tell which contender is highest. Cliffs and ridges block the direct way up, so you have to find a pass, a ridge or a valley that leads there. The generator guarantees at least one walkable route from the landing beach.

**Clouds.** Each contender has a 50% chance of a static cloud cap that covers everything above a set height on that peak. The true summit follows the same rule, so a cloud never gives it away. Inside a cloud your view radius drops to about 8 cells. Surveying a clouded peak ignores the cloud, and the cloud fades during the survey animation and stays gone for the rest of the run.

**The trail of old vardes.** Six to ten old vardes lie along the route to the summit, about 60 to 120 cells apart. Some have fallen over or are hidden in forest. Each one has a note that points to the next, written from the island's real features ("Følg elva til den deler seg, og gå østover"). A note names a feature in the direction of the next varde, and that feature does not have to be in direct line of sight. The trail passes the island's points of interest: ruins, caves and a shipwreck. Vardes you have found and notes you have read show on the map screen, where you can read the notes again.

**Day and night.** A full day lasts about 16 minutes: dawn, about 10 minutes of day, dusk and about 4 minutes of night. A run starts at dawn. When the light falls below a threshold, your character takes out an oil lamp by itself and puts it away at dawn. At night the walking line of sight shrinks to the lamp's light, about 20 cells. Surveying still reaches its full radius by moonlight, and the survey animation lifts the landscape into moonlight as it reveals it.

**The end of a run.** At the summit varde, everything visible from the top becomes surveyed at once. The end screen shows your map next to the real island, the share of land cells you saw, the share you surveyed, how many vardes you built, how many old vardes you found, and the seed. You can export your map as a PNG.

## Look

The style is detailed pixel art: individual pixels everywhere, textured materials, per-pixel lighting, glow and movement. None of it is drawn by hand.

**Text is a pixel font.** All UI text uses Pixel Operator (CC0, `fonts/LICENSE.txt`), set as the default font in `game/theme.tres` and drawn without antialiasing at 960×540, so it scales up with the rest of the frame. It is drawn on a 16px grid with 9px capitals, so at its size of 16 a font pixel is a game pixel, the same as the terrain and the figure. The title is 80, five game pixels to a font pixel. Pixelify Sans came first, but its pixel is 1/11 of an em: at 22 its pixels were twice the art's, at 11 the menus were too small, and its notched corners made B read as G and 5 as S. m6x11 and monogram looked too quirky, and monogram's "a" read as an "o" in the title. Godot's default font, Open Sans, drew smooth letters that didn't match the pixels around them. The landing page uses Literata instead, a serif made for reading on screens, with the title in bold italic. A pixel font in the page's paragraphs was tiring to read, and the backdrop already ties the page to the game.

**The world is 3D, rendered as pixel art.** A terrain mesh built from the heightmap is drawn into a low-resolution viewport and scaled up with integer scaling. You move in 2D on the grid (left, right, forward, back), and the terrain rises and falls under you. The camera is orthographic and looks down at 45° from vertical. It starts facing north, and Q and E turn it a quarter turn left or right in 0.4 s, eased in and out, so the player sees the world turn and keeps their bearings. A fixed north-facing camera hid all the ground around the player on the north side of a massif, because the fade is about 10 world units across and a massif stands up to 100, and north-facing walls never showed. The walking keys follow the camera, so up always walks away from it. Line of sight and fog don't depend on the camera and don't change when it turns. The camera rests only on whole quarters, where it snaps to its pixel grid as at north. Compass words in notes stay true directions, so "gå østover" matches the north-up map whichever way the camera faces. The terrain fade, the check for terrain hiding the figure, the ground details and the dithering all work in the camera's own axes, so they follow the turn without changes. The ground details' sprites keep their drawn light from the upper left at every heading, as they already do when mirrored. Orthographic projection keeps every pixel the same size at any distance.

- Each material (sand, grass, forest, rock, snow, water) has a ramp of 4 to 6 colors. The terrain shader snaps lighting to the ramp steps with ordered dithering instead of blending smoothly. All ramps, including the foam ramp and a warm ramp for the lamp, live in `world/ramps.gd`.
- A directional light is the sun. Its shadows fall across the terrain and move as the sun moves through the day. The sun crosses the sky from east to west in a half circle that stands 45° above the southern horizon at noon. A cast shadow is one ramp step darker than a face turned away from the sun. Under a low sun, flat ground gets little more light than the shade side, and without the extra step its shadows barely show. The debug view has a sun-angle slider to check the shadows from morning to evening. Godot fits the sun's shadow map from the camera out to its far plane, so the camera sits one world unit in front of the nearest terrain it can see. With the camera 3000 units back, the map spread so thin that the player's figure cast no shadow at all.
- Cliffs draw exactly the cells the player can't stand on, from the generator's cliff grid (see Movement). A cliff pixel uses the rock ramp one step darker than walkable rock, with wide horizontal strata about 5 world units apart on all of it, so a wall reads apart from rock the player can climb. Noise moves the cliff's edge by up to 0.2 cells, so it doesn't run in straight steps along the grid. Rock drawn per pixel from the slope's normal disagreed with walking, which checks steps between cells: on seed 0, 23,500 cells drew as cliff with at least two open sides, and 10,600 drew light while blocked on three or more. A noisy edge of 0.15 in the normal's y drew blobs of rock about 10° either side of the limit, and at 0.05 it still didn't follow the cells that walking blocks.
- Water gets its color from depth, with animated ripples and foam at the shore. The sea is part of the terrain mesh, flat at sea level, and the terrain shader reads its depth from the height map. Shallow water reaches the light end of the water ramp, and water about 50 cells out reaches its darkest step. Thin ripple crests, one step lighter, drift across the whole sea. Bands of foam roll in toward the coast over the last 6 cells and thin out farther out, and an unbroken line of foam runs along the coast. The shader measures that distance as the depth over the slope of the sea floor. Measured in depth alone, a wide shallow whose floor stayed near sea level turned to foam all over, in pale blocks that showed the cells. A flat floor now reads as far from the coast, so its foam stays at the shore. Foam has its own ramp, so it shades with the sun like the land. Rivers and lakes use the water ramp at its full reach, with the same ripples. Rivers keep the slope's shading and run over cliffs as waterfalls, and lakes are flat.
- Noise distorts material boundaries so the logic grid never shows.
- Ground details stand on the land, so walking shows the ground passing and their bases show where it lies: tufts, flowers, stones, shrubs, reeds, pebbles and driftwood. Code draws 24 sprites per ground material at startup, each at most 16×16 pixels against the figure's 10×16, with one texel on one screen pixel. The details shader decides on the GPU, for every cell, whether a detail stands there, which sprite it shows and where in the cell it stands, from a hash of the cell and the island seed. Noise gathers the details into clumps with bare ground between. The CPU places none of them, so generation takes no longer. At zoom 40 a screen of them costs about 0.3 ms of GPU time per frame.
  - A sprite's pixels hold a ramp and a step offset instead of a color. The shader lights them with the normal of the ground under them, so a detail takes the sun, the shade side and cast shadows through the ramps like the terrain. Details cast no shadows of their own.
  - A cell holds a detail with a chance that depends on its ground: 45% on grass, forest and marsh, 12% on rock, 8% on the beach and 3% on snow, at the clumps' average. Details skip water, cliffs and ground that faces away from the camera, and they fade with the ground in front of the player, so none floats in the hole.
- Trees are sprites generated at startup too, with 20 to 30 variants per biome, scattered using noise and drawn as billboards, in milestone 6. 3D trees are a `later` idea if billboards look flat.
- When terrain stands between the camera and the player, the terrain in front of the player fades out with a dithered edge. The terrain shader cuts a hole with a radius of 10 world units on screen around the feet, about two thirds of the screen's height across at zoom 40, in terrain that is nearer the camera and higher than the feet. It cuts a pixel only where the view ray behind it comes back out of the ground somewhere past it, which it finds by stepping along the ray through the height map. The mesh is a height field, so a hole into a hill would show the empty space under it. So a ridge or a valley wall in front opens onto the player, and a slope that rises toward the camera from the feet stays. The shadow pass skips the fade, so a faded ridge still casts its shadow. The hole opens only while terrain hides the middle or the top of the figure, which the player checks by stepping from those two points toward the camera over the terrain mesh. It opens and shuts over a quarter of a second. Without that check, a hill beside the player faded even with nothing hidden.
- The lamp is an `OmniLight3D` whose energy and offset flicker from noise, snapped to the lamp ramp with dithering, with a few embers from `GPUParticles3D`.
- `GPUParticles3D` adds birds, falling leaves and mist. Glow in the `WorldEnvironment` gives bloom.
- Unexplored land and sea lie under a dithered fog, and when you see them, the fog clears over about a second.
  - The land under the fog stays at its real height. At first it lay flat at sea level and rose when seen, so that unexplored peaks showed no silhouette, but the rising land looked like a strange dream. Instead, the terrain shader writes fog pixels at the far end of the depth buffer, so land you have seen always draws in front of fog, and a fogged peak in front of it shows no outline and never hides the player. Land you saw earlier shows through a fogged peak until you see the peak. Writing depth turns off the GPU's early depth test, which costs about 0.5 ms per frame in Compatibility and 0.7 ms in Forward+ at 960×540. The player's check for terrain hiding the figure skips unknown cells for the same reason.
  - The fog is lit evenly and takes no cast shadows, and land under it casts none, so the fog doesn't trace the shape of the land under it. Ground details show only once the fog has cleared from under them, because sharp sprites on the thinning fog looked pasted on.
  - Fog covers unknown sea too. Without it, the edge of the fog would trace the island's coast.
  - The fog is the `FOG` ramp in `world/ramps.gd`, grey with a little of the sea's blue. Noise on a flat layer at sea level moves its light up and down 1.5 steps in patches that drift slowly: each fog pixel samples the noise where its view ray meets the sea. Sampled by ground position, the patches ran down the slopes in streaks. Laid out along the screen's axes, they slid across the screen whenever the camera tilted or turned, since the island lies hundreds of units from the origin those axes are measured from.
  - The edge of the fog averages 3 by 3 samples 2 cells apart, warped by noise 1.5 cells, and lies where half of them are clear, with a dither 30% of that range wide. The average rounds the edge and fills single cells the line of sight left out. Without it, the edge followed every such cell in ragged steps. With a dither across the whole range, the edge thinned out over a few cells, and on a steep peak whose top the player had seen, that band showed the unknown slope below as a see-through outline.
  - The title backdrop shows its island with no fog.

**The map screen** is ink on aged paper, drawn at 1024×1024 pixels (one pixel per cell) into a `SubViewport`. It opens as a square sheet 90% of the screen's height, with the run going on behind it on both sides, so the player can walk with the map open. M opens and closes it, and Escape closes it too. The sheet keeps the player in its middle until the player drags it. The mouse wheel or + and - zoom between the zoom that fits the whole island on the sheet and 1, 2 and 4 screen pixels per cell. It keeps its zoom between closing and reopening it within a run, and a new run opens at the first zoom again. North is always up. A red dot marks the player, with a pointer on its edge the way the figure faces and a faint red wedge toward where the camera looks, and a small ink cairn marks each varde the player has built.

- Unknown cells stay blank paper, with a grain of stained pixels and yellowed edges. Seen land is a lighter paper washed 45% toward the middle step of its ground's ramp, with the coast in ink, rivers and lakes in blue ink, and faint dotted outlines around forest and snow. With plain paper and no wash, known land was hard to tell from the unknown around it. The sea the player has seen gets a pale wash, so the coast reads from both sides.
- Surveyed land adds contour lines every 0.04 of the summit's height, 4 world units or 40 m, and every fifth line is drawn in dark ink. At 0.02, with every fifth line two cells wide, the sides of the massifs turned into solid bands of ink, and the wide lines grew thick as the map zoomed in. One fragment shader, `map/map.gdshader`, draws the whole map. A cell is on a line when a neighbor's height falls in a lower band, so each line is one pixel wide and unbroken. Marching squares turned out to be unneeded, since lines drawn per cell show no ragged steps at one pixel per cell. On steep cliffs the lines merge into solid ink.
- While the map is open, it redraws on each frame the knowledge texture changed, because walking adds coast and rivers too, not only surveys. The redraw is one pass of the shader over a million pixels on the GPU.
- The paper and inks are the `MAP` ramps in `world/ramps.gd`. The art style stays open for later.

| Base resolution | 960×540, chosen by eye in milestone 2 over 480×270 and 640×360. It scales exactly to 1080p. Stretch mode `viewport`, integer scaling |
| Logic grid | 1024×1024 cells |
| World size | One world unit per cell. The summit stands 100 units high, chosen by eye in milestone 2 |
| Camera | Orthographic, starts facing north, Q and E turn it in quarter turns of 0.4 s, Q clockwise and E counterclockwise seen from above, so the world swings left on screen for Q. The mouse wheel or + and - step it between 30, 40, 60 and 80, and the tilt from vertical goes with each step: 55°, 45°, 40° and 35°. A step eases over 0.3 s. A run opens at 40 and 45°, and keeps the chosen step for the rest of the run, including after a survey's pull-out. Close in, the steeper tilt makes the slopes read as tall walls, and far out the view flattens toward a map, which suits reading the fog's edge. At 80 the fog's edge at sea level fits on screen. Past 80 the figure shrinks below about 6×12 pixels, and at 70° the slope between the camera and the figure filled most of the screen. | Desktop builds | The `Linux` and `Windows` presets, x86_64, with the game data inside the executable, on Forward+ |

The game plays in the browser at varde.tommeras.no, and the same site links to the Windows and Linux downloads on GitHub Releases. A version tag builds and publishes all three, and the landing page deploys from `main` on its own, as `docs/releasing.md` describes. Windows and Linux are the only desktop builds, because an unsigned macOS app is blocked on its first launch and notarizing it needs a paid Apple developer account. The web build has only the Compatibility renderer, so every shader has to look the same there as in Forward+. Three settings broke that. Compatibility doesn't decode a `source_color` texture read with `texelFetch`, so the ramp textures hold linear colors in half floats. HDR 2D added a second sRGB curve, so it is off. At the sun's default normal bias of 2, Compatibility drew shadow acne as dark polygons on sunlit slopes, so the bias is 5. With both changes, a frame in Compatibility differs from Forward+ in about 0.1% of its pixels, at dither and shadow edges. An island takes about 1.6 times as long to generate in Chrome as natively.

The terrain shader generates detail finer than a cell from grid-sized textures. The terrain mesh comes in chunks of 64×64 cells, because a single mesh of 1M cells is too heavy. The renderer skips the chunks off screen, which at the game's zoom of 40 cells leaves 2 to 6 of the 256. Each chunk also has a coarser level with every second vertex, which Godot draws when a pixel covers 2 cells or more. The camera is orthographic, so the level follows the zoom instead of the distance from the camera, and all chunks on screen draw at the same level and meet without cracks.

## Generator pipeline

Each step reads the arrays the earlier steps produced. All data lives in packed arrays of length 1024×1024.

1. **Height.** Layered noise, multiplied by a falloff from the center so the edges sink into the sea, makes the lowland and low hills. On it the generator raises 6 to 9 massifs, each 2 to 4 ridges of uplift that branch from one point and fall away toward their ends, inland and apart from each other. River erosion then cuts the massifs into ranges, with branching valleys, sharp ridges and one clear top each. It runs on a 512×512 grid: water routes downhill to a random lower neighbor weighted by slope, and each cell is rebuilt from the lowland up with a slope that shrinks as more water drains through it. The ridge of each massif that points most toward the island's middle is soft rock, which wears down to gentler slopes, so most massifs get a side the player can walk up. Last, fine ridged noise adds rock ribs to the high ground, and a curve keeps the lowland low and runs straight from 0.6 of the summit up, so the summit is exactly 1.0. Shapes stamped on as final heights, with cones, carved ways up and terraces, looked like geometry, and so did round domes of uplift, which eroded into cones. With erosion on a 256 grid the massifs came out as smooth, rounded slopes, and a plain power curve made their upper slopes too steep to walk.
2. **Moisture.** A second noise layer.
3. **Material.** A lookup table from height and moisture to beach, grass, forest, marsh, rock or snow. A patch of snow smaller than 32 cells takes the ground of the band below, because bumps and wet spots just over the snow line lay as specks on the bare rock. A flood fill then marks the main island, the largest connected land area. Islets off its coast stay as scenery the player can see but not reach, and stats, peaks, vardes and points of interest use only the main island.
4. **Rivers.** About 30 springs, at least 60 cells apart, lie on the main island's high ground. Each spring sends a river downhill, at each step to a random lower neighbor weighted by slope, because always taking the steepest one ran the rivers in straight lines along the grid's 8 directions. A river that runs into a pit fills it up to the lowest point on its rim and runs on from there. The filled pit stays flat in the height map as a lake. A pit below sea level of up to 500 cells that land closes off from the sea counts too, and turns into a lake on beach. Erosion leaves such pits on the flat lowland by the coast, and as sea they showed as pale pools of foam inland. Larger basins below sea level stay sea. A river that meets an earlier one joins it and ends there, so the rivers form branching networks. Pits get filled only where a river finds one, because filling the whole grid first takes seconds in GDScript. The generator keeps a mask of the lake cells, because lakes block the player and rivers don't. Rivers widen downstream with the square root of the river cells above them, from 1 cell to 5.
5. **Features.** Find nameable things: peaks with their prominence, lakes, river forks, coves and the largest forest. Before the start is placed, the generator marks the cliffs (see Movement). Once the start is placed, it checks the way up to each peak. It searches from the peak's top and the cells beside it to the land the start reaches for the path that crosses the fewest cliff cells, and raises each such cell and its lower neighbors until no step around it is too steep. It only raises cells, never to the height of the cell above, so no top moves. It never raises a river or lake cell, so water doesn't run uphill, and it goes round cliffs on or next to a river or the sea. A mend for one peak can cut off another, so it checks all the peaks again until a pass raises nothing. Afterwards the generator redoes the materials, the peaks and the cliffs from the new heights. On seeds 0 to 9 it raises 3 to 725 cells per island. Searching from anywhere in the survey zone left the summit of seed 3638347899 walled in by cliff cells, with its only open zone cell 3 cells east of the top. A way carved all the way up made straight pathways, so the terrain itself has to leave the way open nearly everywhere.
6. **Summit and route.** Pick the contenders and rescale them into a narrow height band, pick the true summit, place the clouds, place the landing beach, and find a walkable route between beach and summit.
7. **Points of interest and vardes.** Place ruins, caves and the shipwreck by rule, and the old vardes along the route.
8. **Notes.** Write each varde's note from the features between it and the next varde.

Generation runs on a background thread behind a short "charting the island" screen and may take at most 10 seconds on the dev machine. If a step pushes it over, profile it before moving it to C# or a GDExtension.

## Runtime systems

- **Line of sight** runs on the CPU and is recomputed only when the player enters a new cell. The 10 cells around the player always count as seen, because from a rounded top the rays pass over the slope just below it. On the summit of seed 0 only 49 of the 121 cells within 5 were seen, and at 4 cells the softened edge of the fog still covered the ground underfoot. `player/line_of_sight.gd` casts one ray per cell of the view's rim, 2π times the radius, from an eye 8 world units above the ground. At the figure's height, 1.5, the player had to stand at the very edge of a cliff to clear the fog below it, which felt wrong from a camera that looks down. At 8 the player sees about twice as many cells on a massif's slopes, 5,000 against 2,500, for the same cost. Each ray samples every half cell and keeps the steepest slope from the eye so far, and a cell shows when its ground reaches that slope. At whole-cell samples, rays skipped 168 of the 4,800 cells inside the sea-level radius where they clipped a cell's corner. The radius grows in a straight line with the ground's height, from 40 cells at sea level to 120 on the summit. The sea is marked too, so the fog can cover the unknown sea. A cast takes 4.4 ms at sea level and 34 ms on the summit natively, and 5.9 ms and 45 ms in Chrome, so it runs on a `WorkerThreadPool` task and marks its cells when it finishes. Marking the 10,000 to 27,000 cells seen from a peak takes 0.4 to 1 ms on the main thread. A survey casts the same rays at twice the radius, which takes 80 to 180 ms natively for the 60,000 to 126,000 cells it reaches from the peaks of seeds 0 to 2, the most from the summit, so it runs on a worker too. Marking all of them in one frame took 6 to 11 ms, so the survey splits them into 48 bands by distance and marks each band as the ring reaches it.
- **Knowledge** is one byte per cell: unknown (0), seen (1) or surveyed (2), and the map is drawn from it. `world/knowledge.gd` holds the bytes and an R8 texture of them, which both terrain materials carry as `knowledge_map`. Marking a cell only raises its level. The texture uploads once a frame, and only when a mark changed a cell. A full upload of the 1 MB grid costs 0.05 to 0.11 ms of CPU time natively (Forward+ and Compatibility) and 0.11 to 0.14 ms in Chrome, with no change in frame time even when it runs every frame, so there is no partial upload. The debug view's "Vis alt" button marks every cell surveyed. God mode (G) shows the whole island as surveyed, on the terrain and the map, without marking any cell, and line of sight pauses while it is on, so leaving it shows the fog as the player left it. A survey in god mode still marks what it reaches. God mode (`game/god_mode.gd`, debug builds only) also lets the player walk through anything at 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16 times the walking speed (keys 1 to 5, ×8 at first), zooms with the mouse wheel from 10 to 400 cells, jumps to the nearest unsurveyed peak with T, and shows every unsurveyed peak's zone ring at a fixed size on screen. A panel in the top left corner shows the seed, the cell, its height, slope, moisture and material, whether it is walkable, how many peaks are surveyed and where the nearest unsurveyed one lies. It gives heights and distances in meters at a map scale of 10 m per cell (`IslandData.METERS_PER_CELL`), so the island is about 10 km across and the summit stands at 1000 moh. The figure isn't drawn to that scale: it would be 15 m tall, and at the walking speed of 5 cells/s it would cover 180 km/h.
  - For the fog, Knowledge also keeps the time each cell was first seen, as a float per cell in an RF texture (`reveal_map`), and the shaders clear the fog from each cell over the second after it. The clock is Knowledge's own, advanced by the terrain node each frame, so the clearing pauses with the game and follows a movie's fixed frame rate. The upload of both grids, 5 MB, costs about 1.4 ms of CPU time in Forward+ and 2.9 ms in Compatibility, only on frames where the player learns new cells. A small texture that moves with the player and covers only the cells whose fog is still clearing would cut that, if it shows in the frame time.
  - The first cast of a run, from the start, runs on the main thread when the player is placed, and its fog counts as already cleared, so the run doesn't open in fog.
- **Movement** is free movement checked against the grid, with no physics shapes for terrain. `generator/walkability.gd` holds the step check, and the route guarantee in generator step 6 uses the same check, so the player can never walk into a valley with no way out.
  - The sea and lakes block. Rivers can be waded. Blocking rivers too left 90 to 97% of the land reachable on seeds 0 to 4, but meant a detour round a spring every hundred cells or so along the coast.
  - Cliffs are cells. The generator marks every land cell with a step to a neighbor steeper than 60° as cliff, about 1.73 world units per cell at a summit of 100, and the same over the length of a corner step. Both cells of a steep step are cliff, so a wall is about two cells thick, its foot and its top. The player can't step onto a cliff, as they can't step into a lake, so every step between two open cells is walkable, and the terrain shader draws the same grid. With steps checked between cells along each axis instead, a cell at the foot of a wall drew as cliff with most steps open, and the player zigzagged up diagonal faces of about 68°. Cliffs cover 12 to 26% of the main island on seeds 0 to 9, and the start reaches 97 to 99% of the rest. At 63° they covered 8 to 19% and at 66° 4 to 12%. At 72° the player climbed spires and walked up walls. At 45° the eroded massifs were too steep to walk up on almost every side. In the prototype, with nothing carved, 1 of 15 peaks on seeds 0 and 2 could be reached at 45°, and 54 of 57 on seeds 0 to 7 at 60°. A corner step also needs both cells beside it open, so the player can't slip between two cliff corners. If the summit turns out too easy to reach, milestone 5 adds cliffs in the generator rather than invisible walls.
  - The player walks 5 cells per second, in 8 directions with diagonals as fast as straight lines. It takes 0.15 s to reach full speed and to stop. At 6 cells per second, starting and stopping dead, the figure looked like it was sprinting and floated, and at 3.5 the walks between massifs, often 100 to 200 cells, felt slow. The camera follows the player's height with a lag of 0.8 s, so the figure rises on screen while it climbs and sinks while it descends. With the camera locked on the feet, walking up a slope only slid the ground diagonally past a figure that never moved on screen. A move into a step the check refuses keeps only its east-west or north-south part, so the player slides along walls. The figure stands on the terrain mesh's triangles, not a smoothed height, so its feet meet the ground on steep slopes.
  - The figure is a 3D model that code builds from spheres, capsules and boxes, drawn in the same low-resolution view as the terrain. It has a round head with eyes and hair painted on by the shader, a beanie, a coat, a pack, and arms and legs that swing from the shoulders and hips and bend at the elbows and knees. At zoom 40 it is about 12×25 pixels. Its shader snaps light to the figure ramps with the terrain's dithering, so it takes the sun's light and casts a real shadow. Light dithers only over the middle 30% of the gap between two steps, because dithering across the whole gap, as on the terrain, turned the coat into a checkerboard. The camera looks down at 45°, so it sees more of the crown than of the face. A cap over the whole crown hid the face down to the eyes, so the beanie sits high and to the back. The figure faces the nearest of 8 directions, and the legs make a full cycle every 2.4 cells walked, about 4 steps a second. The body drops 0.1 units, about 2 pixels, at each footfall and leans 0.12 radians forward, both scaled by the share of full speed, so the figure settles as it stops. It replaced a billboard of 10×16 pixels whose arms and legs were single pixels at zoom 40. A 2D sprite drawn from a skeleton was sharper but flat, and it would need its own views for each camera angle in the survey spin. The figure uses the depth test, so terrain in front hides it until the fade opens. It sits 0.3 world units nearer the camera than its feet, which doesn't move it on screen but keeps the boots out of a slope that rises to the side. At 0 the boots showed through the silhouette on steep rock. Where terrain or a ground detail hides part of the figure, that part draws as a flat silhouette in the coat's lightest red, so the player can tell standing behind a crest from standing in front of it. The coat's darkest step was the color of the figure in shade and didn't show. The figure marks the stencil buffer where it shows, and the silhouette draws only where that mark is missing, so a part hidden behind another part of the figure, such as the pack behind the torso, draws nothing. The camera follows the figure, and both snap to the same pixel grid, so the figure moves on screen only in whole pixels, and only while the camera catches up on its height.
  - The player walks at the same speed on every kind of ground for now. Slower forest, marsh and rivers are left for playtesting to decide (#52).
  - The player starts on a random beach cell of the main island that reaches at least 20,000 cells, so a cove walled in by cliffs can't trap them. From there 98 to 99% of the main island's open land, off lakes and cliffs, is reachable on seeds 0 to 4. Milestone 5 replaces this start with the landing beach.

## Project layout

```
generator/   pure GDScript classes: noise, rivers, features, route, vardes, notes -> IslandData
game/        the title screen, which is the main scene, and the game scene for one run
world/       terrain mesh chunks, the terrain shader, the camera, the sun, the island loader, decoration scattering, the color ramps
player/      movement checked against the grid, and the 3D figure
map/         the map screen and contour drawing
notes/       one note template file per language
debug/       seed input, layer toggles, free camera, resolution, zoom, tilt and height sliders, sun angle, reveal-all
tests/       headless generator tests
```

## Milestones

1. **Generator and debug view.** Generate an island from a seed and show the height, moisture and material layers as images.
2. **The look.** It starts with a 3D view of a real island with a free camera, where you choose the base resolution, the zoom, the tilt and the summit height by eye. Then the terrain mesh, the terrain shader with materials, sun and shadows, and water. This step decides how the game feels, and it is done when 5 seeds look right.
3. **Walking.** The title screen and the game scene, then the player, camera, walkability, the terrain fade in front of the player and the ground details.
4. **Exploring.** Line of sight, the knowledge texture, fog that clears where you look, peaks and surveying, and the map screen with contour lines.
5. **The summit.** Contenders and clouds, the summit varde, the trail with its notes, and the end screen.
6. **Polish.** Generated trees, the day cycle with moving shadows and the lamp, particles, glow and sound.

Work is tracked as GitHub issues, one GitHub milestone per milestone above. Ideas for after these milestones carry the `later` label, and values to adjust after playtesting carry `tuning`.

## Language

All in-game text is in Norwegian bokmål. Godot's translation system carries the UI text, so English can come later as one more column in the translation CSV.

Varde notes are generated, and Norwegian and English need different grammar, such as definite endings ("elva") and gendered words ("en elv", "et vann"). The note templates therefore live in one `ConfigFile` per language, for example `notes/nb.cfg`. Each noun lists its indefinite and definite forms, each feature type has a list of templates with `{placeholder}` slots, and directions are 8 compass words. Adding English means writing a new template file, not changing the generator. A test checks that every template loads as a string, because `ConfigFile` turns a malformed value into `null` without an error.

Notes use common nouns ("vannet", "elva") for now. Generated place names are a `later` idea.
